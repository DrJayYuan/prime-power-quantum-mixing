EXACT UNIFORM MIXING · RESEARCH DRAFT · 28 NOVEMBER 2026

Exact uniform mixing on
odd-prime-power quantum paths

Jing Yuan, ChatGPT

Prepare a particle at a site, let it move along a uniform quantum chain, and measure its position once at a randomly drawn time. For chains of N = pa − 1 sites, with p an odd prime and a ≥ 2, we prove that one finite distribution of integer readout times makes every site equally likely, irrespective of the starting site. At most N²/4 + 1 times are needed. For eight sites, we certify a schedule with 17 explicit times.

What is randomized in each run?

Fix one schedule before the experiment and use it for every starting site v. Uniformity means that, over repeated runs from any such v, the probability of reading each site u is 1/N.

Let A be the adjacency matrix of the path PN, with one unit of hopping strength on each edge and ℏ = 1. For Hamiltonian −A, write U(t) = exp(itA) and M(t)uv = |U(t)uv|². If the schedule draws tj with probability wj, the observed transition matrix is the weighted average of the matrices M(tj).

Theorem. For an odd prime p and integer a ≥ 2, let q = pa and N = q − 1. There are at most N²/4 + 1 nonnegative integer times tj and positive weights wj summing to 1 such that

∑j wj M(tj) = J/N,

where J is the all-ones matrix.

Why work with phases?

Each energy mode is a standing-wave pattern on the chain. As time passes, the modes acquire different phases, and a site probability depends on how they interfere. Averaging over the readout time lets us tune their pairwise interference. The challenge is that one time fixes all the phases at once. We first design a distribution on the combinations that the phases can approach, then show how to reproduce its relevant averages exactly using finitely many actual times.

Baptista, Coutinho and Marques constructed schedules for three and four sites [1]. Kang ruled out eleven sites [2] and later classified several families of lengths [3]. The composite odd prime powers treated here fall outside those families. We use Kang’s target for the average interference and construct a phase distribution using the prime-power energy relations.

1. Start with eight sites

For eight sites, write q = 9. The four even-numbered modes have energies αj = 2 cos(2πj/9) for j = 1, 2, 3, 4. Their phases at time t are zj = exp(itαj). The odd-numbered modes have opposite energies and conjugate phases. For distinct modes, the target average cosine of the phase difference is −1/8 when both modes are even or both are odd, and zero when one is even and one is odd. Section 2 derives this target.

Three of the even-mode phases are tied together. Put ζ = exp(2πi/9). The sum ζ + ζ⁴ + ζ⁷ is zero, so taking twice its real part gives α1 + α2 + α4 = 0. Thus z1z2z4 = 1. Section 3 shows that this is the only independent constraint on the phases in the closure of the orbit. We can treat {1, 2, 4} as a group and leave z3 free.

Two ways to draw the phases

Let ω = exp(2πi/3). In the first draw, shuffle the three roots {1, ω, ω²} and assign them to the group’s three coordinates. Their product is 1. Two different members of the group have an average product zjzk of −1/2, since the quotient is equally likely to be ω or ω². Draw the free phase independently from the same roots. Its mean is zero, so it has zero correlation with the group.

The second draw supplies the missing negative correlation with the free phase. Give all three group members phase 1 and the free coordinate phase −1. The group product is still 1.

Why go past −1/8?

Multiply every coordinate by the same independent uniform cube root. This leaves the group product and all the correlations in the table unchanged. It also makes the averages of zjzk zero because the multiplier is squared. These products control the opposite-parity moments.

We now have a stronger negative correlation than needed. Use this draw with probability 5/8. With the remaining probability 3/8, draw independently uniform angles for z1, z2 and z3, and set z4 = (z1z2)−1. This second component covers every allowed neighborhood and contributes zero to the off-diagonal moments. The resulting same-parity correlation is (5/8)(−1/5) = −1/8. Section 5 uses this extra spread to recover a finite schedule of actual times.

2. Which averages give a uniform readout?

Let q = N + 1. The path has distinct energies θk = 2 cos(πk/q) and orthonormal modes with components ψk(u) = (2/q)1/2 sin(πku/q), for 1 ≤ k, u ≤ N. Write Ek for the projector onto mode k, and ∘ for entrywise matrix multiplication. Expanding the spectral formula U(t) = ∑k exp(itθk) Ek shows how the phase differences enter the measured probabilities.

𝔼M(t) = D + ∑k ≠ l 𝔼 cos((θk − θl)t) (Ek ∘ El), D = ∑k Ek ∘ Ek.

The sum is over ordered pairs. The sine terms cancel when the two indices are exchanged, leaving the cosines shown above.

Sufficient moment condition. To obtain the uniform matrix J/N, it suffices to give every pair of distinct same-parity modes average cosine −1/N and every opposite-parity pair average cosine 0. [3]

Proof of the sufficient condition

Let R be the reflection matrix taking site u to q − u, and let I be the identity. To compute Duv, sum the product of squared sines in its definition. With x = πku/q and y = πkv/q, the summand before the factor 1/q² is

4 sin² x sin² y = 1 − cos(2x) − cos(2y) + ½ cos(2(x − y)) + ½ cos(2(x + y)).

We can include k = 0 in the sum because the sine product there is zero. The cosine sum over k = 0, …, q − 1 is zero unless its frequency is a multiple of q, in which case it is q. The single-site frequencies u and v never qualify. The difference qualifies just when u = v, and the sum just when u + v = q. Hence D = (2J + I + R)/(2q).

Reflection gives Rψk = (−1)k+1ψk. The odd- and even-index projector sums are therefore Podd = (I + R)/2 and Peven = (I − R)/2. Adding the two entrywise squares gives

∑k ≡ l (mod 2) Ek ∘ El = Podd ∘ Podd + Peven ∘ Peven = (I + R)/2.

Subtract D from this sum to remove its diagonal pairs. Inserting the proposed moments into the expansion yields

𝔼M(t) = D − (1/N) ((I + R)/2 − D) = (q/N)D − (I + R)/(2N) = J/N.

3. What can one clock reach?

We now find the general counterpart of the product constraint z1z2z4 = 1. Put s = q/p, m = (q − 1)/2 and ζ = exp(2πi/q). The even-mode energies are αj = θ2j = ζj + ζ−j for 1 ≤ j ≤ m. The odd energies are the negatives, with θq−2j = −αj.

Each index j stands for the pair of residues ±j modulo q. Reduce these pairs modulo s. Each nonzero pair ±r gives a full block Br of p indices, since either of the two residues has p lifts and the lifts pair off by sign. There are (s − 1)/2 full blocks. The indices divisible by s form one short block of size (p − 1)/2. Altogether there are K = (s + 1)/2 blocks.

The energy relations are block sums

For an integer relation ∑j cjαj = 0, form P(X) = ∑j cj(Xj + Xq−j). Its value at ζ is zero. The minimal polynomial of ζ over ℚ is

Φq(X) = 1 + Xs + X2s + ⋯ + X(p−1)s.

Thus P = QΦq with deg Q < s, since deg P < q. Multiplication repeats the coefficients of Q in p disjoint stretches of length s. Every coefficient of P therefore depends only on its exponent modulo s. The residue-zero coefficient vanishes because P(0) = 0. Symmetry under exponent reversal j ↔ q − j identifies residues r and −r. It follows that cj is constant on each full block and zero on the short block. Conversely, the polynomial for a single full-block sum is (Xr + Xs−r)Φq. Hence those block sums generate all the integer relations.

From energy relations to allowed phase combinations

Let T be the set of unit phases with ∏j∈B zj = 1 in every full block. The short block is free. By Kronecker’s approximation theorem, the orbit zj(t) = exp(itαj) for integer t is dense in the compact subgroup determined by the relations ∑j cjαj ∈ 2πℤ. Here the sum is algebraic, so the transcendence of π forces it to be zero. The only constraints are therefore the full-block products, and the closure is T. Nonnegative integers give the same closure. In a compact group, positive powers recur arbitrarily close to the identity, so negative powers are limits of nonnegative ones.

We can now design a distribution on T using the product constraints alone. Section 5 returns to the physical clock.

4. Generalize the two draws

We want the same correlation for every pair of distinct even-mode phases. The first draw makes it negative within a block. The second makes it negative across blocks. As in the eight-site example, a mixture balances the two.

First draw. Shuffle roots independently in each block

Put ω = exp(2πi/p). Draw each block’s coordinates without replacement from the p roots, in uniform order and independently between blocks. The product of all roots is ωp(p−1)/2 = 1, so every full block satisfies the constraint. Every coordinate has mean zero, giving zero correlation across blocks. Within a block, sum over the p(p − 1) ordered pairs of distinct roots. The sum over all pairs is zero, and the diagonal pairs contribute p, so the average over distinct pairs is −1/(p − 1). This applies to the short block as well whenever it has two or more coordinates.

Second draw. Give each block a common phase

The within-block correlation is now 1. We need the across-block correlation −h with h > 1/(s − 1). If s = 3, use the eight-site choice 1 on the full block and −1 on the singleton, so h = 1. For s > 3, build a multiset of K phases. Since 2K = s + 1 and p divides s, there are ⌊K/p⌋ complete copies of the p roots plus a remainder of (p + 1)/2 phases. Use the even powers 1, ω², ω⁴, …, ωp−1 for that remainder.

S = 1 + ω² + ⋯ + ωp−1 = (1 − ωp+1)/(1 − ω²) = 1/(1 + ω),   |S| = 1/(2 cos(π/p)) ≤ 1.

Here S is also the sum of the entire multiset because each complete copy sums to zero. Assign its entries to the blocks by a uniform random permutation. Each shared phase is a pth root, so each full block still has product 1. Summing over two distinct slots as above gives

−h = (|S|² − K)/[K(K − 1)],   h ≥ 1/K > 1/(s − 1).

Balance the correlations and leave room for a uniform component

Choose the second draw with probability λ and the first with probability 1 − λ. The within-block correlation is λ − (1 − λ)/(p − 1) and the across-block correlation is −λh. Equating them gives

λ = 1/[p + (p − 1)h],   c = λh > 1/(ps − 1) = 1/N,

where the inequality follows by multiplying through by the positive denominators and using (s − 1)h > 1. The common conjugate-pair correlation is −c. A common independent uniform pth root multiplier preserves this correlation and every full-block product. It makes all unconjugated pair means zero because the square of the multiplier has mean zero. Restoring the odd modes as conjugates, the resulting law has moment −c for distinct same-parity modes and 0 across parities.

5. How approximation gives an exact schedule

Use the law from Section 4 with probability 1/(Nc) and the uniform law on T (normalized Haar measure) otherwise. One way to draw from the uniform law is to choose independent uniform angles for every free coordinate and all but one coordinate of each full block. Set the last one in each full block to make the product 1. This law gives positive probability to every nonempty open region of T.

For distinct modes, the phase quotient is a nontrivial character of T. On the time orbit it is exp(it(θk − θl)), and the nonzero algebraic gap cannot be a multiple of 2π. A nontrivial character has zero Haar mean: translation leaves the mean unchanged while multiplying it by a phase different from 1. The mixture thus has the target moments of Section 2. Because c > 1/N, its uniform component has strictly positive weight.

Let F(z) list the real parts of the phase quotients for distinct modes. Since θq−k = −θk, the pairs (k, l) and (q − l, q − k) give the same coordinate. Keep the representatives k < l, k + l ≤ q. The m(2m − 1) unordered pairs include m fixed pairs and the rest occur in twos. This leaves m² coordinates, with m = N/2.

Every choice of readout time gives one point on this moment curve, and mixing times gives a convex combination of points. Let C = conv F(T), which is compact because F(T) is compact in finite dimension. The target y is the average of F under our mixture. If y were on the relative boundary, there would be a supporting hyperplane through it with some point of C strictly on one side. Some phase tuple, and hence an open neighborhood of it, would then map strictly to that side. Full support gives that neighborhood positive probability, moving the average strictly off the hyperplane. Thus y lies in the relative interior of C.

Choose a small simplex around y in the affine hull of C. Its vertices belong to C, so each is a finite mixture of phase-image points. By density and continuity, replace those points by nearby integer-time points. The resulting vertices stay in the same affine hull and can be made arbitrarily close. Since the original barycentric coordinates of y are all positive, sufficiently small changes keep y in the new simplex. This realizes the target exactly as a mixture of actual times. Carathéodory’s theorem reduces the mixture to at most m² + 1 times. The sufficient moment condition then gives J/N.

How the certificate works

Form the 17 × 17 matrix B with one column per listed time tj. Its first row consists of ones. The remaining sixteen rows contain cos((θk − θl)tj) for the representatives k < l, k + l ≤ 9. Set the first entry of b to 1 and the remaining entries to −1/8 for same-parity pairs and 0 otherwise. Then Bw = b encodes both normalization and the desired moments.

The verification script encloses the trigonometric entries in intervals. It rounds an approximate inverse C and weight vector v to exact dyadic rationals. With the infinity norm, the verified bounds are

‖I − CB‖∞ < 1.3 × 10−11,    ‖w − v‖∞ < 4.5 × 10−13.

The first inequality implies that CB is invertible by the Neumann series, so B is invertible. The second follows by bounding ‖C(b − Bv)‖∞ and dividing by 1 − ‖I − CB‖∞. Every exact weight exceeds 0.02022. A separate floating-point diagonalization and the dyadic weights gave a largest deviation of 3.81 × 10−13 from 1/8 across all 64 entries. The general theorem bounds the number of settings. This example also specifies how long to wait at each setting.
